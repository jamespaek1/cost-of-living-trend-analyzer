Data notes

Country data is fetched live from the free World Bank Open Data API: annual CPI inflation history, GNI per capita (PPP), and the price-level ratio (how much prices in your country are of US levels at market exchange rates, PPP-based). Resolution is country-level — the city field is a display label, since free city-level price APIs don't exist (Numbeo's is licensed; see README to wire it). World Bank series are annual, so the "latest" year is typically the prior calendar year; the dashed line is the same damped-trend model applied to your country. The page auto-rechecks all live sources every 15 minutes and on tab focus.

Purchasing power — expensive ≠ unaffordable

The same price tag bites harder on a smaller paycheck. This divides each city's average net wage by its cost of living, indexed to New York = 100. Higher means locals can buy more.

Data notes

Purchasing power = average net monthly wage ÷ cost-of-living index, rebased to New York = 100 — the same idea as Numbeo's Local Purchasing Power Index. Wage figures are approximate net-salary proxies; swap in Numbeo's API or World Bank GNI-per-capita (PPP) for live values. The point isn't the exact rank — it's the reframe: a city can top the cost charts yet stay livable on local wages.

Model notes

Default spending shares approximate BLS CPI relative-importance weights; your personal rate is the share-weighted average of category year-over-year changes — the same arithmetic the CPI uses, with your weights. The scenario is first-order: it moves only the energy term (≈7% weight) and holds everything else fixed, ignoring second-round effects (energy feeding into food, transport, etc.), so treat it as a lower bound on the pass-through. The trend chart's dashed line is a damped-trend (Holt) exponential-smoothing projection fit in-browser to the plotted series, with an ~80% band from in-sample residuals — pure momentum, no knowledge of news. All computation is client-side; no data leaves the page.
